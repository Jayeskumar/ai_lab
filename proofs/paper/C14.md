# Cycle C14 — Written proofs (graphs as quantities: the local graph products)

**Setting.** Graphs are finite simple graphs. For vertices x, y of G let
rel_G(x, y) = E if x = y, A if x ~ y, N otherwise. A *local product* is a set
S of cells (r, s) ∈ {E, A, N}² ∖ {(E, E)}; the graph G ∗ H = G ∗_S H has vertex
set V(G) × V(H) and (g, h) ~ (g', h') iff (rel_G(g, g'), rel_H(h, h')) ∈ S.
There are 2⁸ = 256 local products, encoded by an integer `code` (bit i ↔ cell i
in the order EA, EN, AE, AA, AN, NE, NA, NN; `systems/graph_products.py`).
Named examples: Cartesian {EA, AE} (code 5), direct {AA} (8), strong
{EA, AE, AA} (13), lexicographic {EA, AE, AA, AN} (29), symmetric difference
(XOR) {EA, AE, AN, NA} (85), co-normal {EA, AE, AA, AN, NA} (93).

For a product S define φ_S : {E,A,N}² → {E,A,N} by φ_S(E, E) = E,
φ_S(r, s) = A if (r, s) ∈ S and N otherwise. By definition
rel_{G∗H}((g,h),(g',h')) = φ_S(rel_G(g,g'), rel_H(h,h')).

**Matrices.** For a graph G with adjacency matrix A put R_E = I, R_A = A,
R_N = J − I − A. Then R_E + R_A + R_N = J, the three matrices have disjoint
supports, and the adjacency matrix of G ∗ H is
    A(G ∗ H) = Σ_{(r,s) ∈ S} R_r(G) ⊗ R_s(H).                         (0)
The nine matrices R_r(G) ⊗ R_s(H) have pairwise disjoint supports, and for
G = H = P₃ (the path a – b – c) every one of them is nonzero (E: (a,a),
A: (a,b), N: (a,c)).

standard axioms only).

## Theorem 1 (CLM-C14-01) — associativity is a finite identity on relations

For a local product S the following are equivalent:

1. for all graphs G, H, K the canonical bijection ((g,h),k) ↦ (g,(h,k)) is an
   isomorphism (G ∗ H) ∗ K → G ∗ (H ∗ K);
2. it is an isomorphism for G = H = K = P₃;
3. φ_S(φ_S(r₁, r₂), r₃) = φ_S(r₁, φ_S(r₂, r₃)) for all r₁, r₂, r₃.

Likewise the swap (g,h) ↦ (h,g) is an isomorphism G ∗ H → H ∗ G for all G, H
iff it is one for G = H = P₃ iff S is symmetric ((r,s) ∈ S ⇔ (s,r) ∈ S).

*Proof.* (3 ⇒ 1) The relation of ((g,h),k) and ((g',h'),k') in (G∗H)∗K is
φ_S(φ_S(r₁, r₂), r₃) with rᵢ the relations in the factors, and the relation of
their images in G∗(H∗K) is φ_S(r₁, φ_S(r₂, r₃)); adjacency is "relation = A".
(1 ⇒ 2) is trivial. (2 ⇒ 3) Choose in P₃ pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ) with
rel(xᵢ, yᵢ) = rᵢ. If (r₁, r₂, r₃) = (E, E, E) both sides of (3) equal E. Otherwise
both sides differ from E (φ_S(x, y) = E only for x = y = E), so both lie in
{A, N}, and (2) applied to ((x₁,x₂),x₃), ((y₁,y₂),y₃) says that one equals A
iff the other does; hence they are equal. The commutative statement is the same
argument with pairs. ∎ — Lean: `rel_lprod`, `assoc_iso`, `assoc_iso_nonempty`,
`assoc_of_P3` (both directions, for arbitrary vertex types).

## Theorem 2 (CLM-C14-02) — the twenty associative products

Exactly 20 of the 256 local products are associative, with codes
0, 1, 4, 5, 8, 13, 28, 29, 31, 73, 77, 85, 93, 109, 127, 141, 221, 223, 253, 255.
Ten are commutative: empty (0), Cartesian (5), direct (8), strong (13),
symmetric difference (85), co-normal (93), {EA EN AE AA AN NE NA} (127),
{EA AE AA NN} (141), {EA AE AA AN NA NN} (221), complete (255). Eight have the
two-sided unit K₁: Cartesian, strong, lexicographic (29) and its transpose (77),
symmetric difference, co-normal, 141 and 221. The ten associative products that
depend on the edges of both factors are 5, 8, 13, 29, 77, 85, 93, 127, 141, 221;
eight of them are commutative.

*Proof.* By Theorem 1 associativity is the finite check (3) over 27 triples;
it was evaluated for all 256 codes in Lean by kernel computation (`assoc_codes`,
`comm_assoc_codes`) and independently in Python, where it also agreed with
direct graph computations (P₃³ and 40 random triples per code; EXP-C14-01) and
with a third, library-independent implementation (EXP-C14-06). Units: |V(U ∗ G)|
= |V(U)||V(G)| forces |V(U)| = 1, and K₁ ∗ G is G, its complement, K_n or
the edgeless graph according to which of EA, EN lies in S; K₁ ∗ G ≅ G for all G
iff EA ∈ S and EN ∉ S (test G = P₃). ∎

*The symmetry group.* Transposition (S ↦ Sᵀ) and complement-conjugation
(G ∗' H := complement(Ḡ ∗ H̄), i.e. exchange A ↔ N in both coordinates and
complement S) are commuting involutions preserving associativity (conjugation
by the involution G ↦ Ḡ, which fixes K₁, is an isomorphism of the product
structures). The 20 products form 8 orbits: {0,255}, {1,4,223,253}, {5,221},
{8,127}, {13,93}, {28,31,73,109}, {29,77}, {85,141}.

*Literature.* Imrich & Izbicki (1975) proved that there are exactly twenty
associative products of this kind; Nowakowski & Rall (1996) record that ten of
them depend on both factors and eight of those are commutative — consistent
with the list above. REDISCOVERY.

## Theorem 3 (CLM-C14-03) — up to isomorphism is no weaker

A local product satisfies (G ∗ H) ∗ K ≅ G ∗ (H ∗ K) for all graphs iff it is
(strictly) associative; it satisfies G ∗ H ≅ H ∗ G for all graphs iff S is
symmetric.

*Proof (computer-assisted).* "If" is Theorem 1. For each of the 236
non-associative codes EXP-C14-02 exhibits graphs G, H, K with at most three
vertices each (product orders 2, 4 or 8) such that (G ∗ H) ∗ K and G ∗ (H ∗ K)
have different edge counts or degree sequences, and for each of the 224
non-symmetric codes a pair G, H with ≤ 3 vertices such that G ∗ H and H ∗ G
differ in edge count or degree sequence. These certificates are checkable by
hand and were re-checked by an independent implementation (EXP-C14-06). ∎

## Theorem 4 (CLM-C14-04) — multiplicative characters

Call c = (c_E, c_A, c_N) ∈ ℂ³ with c_A ≠ c_N a *multiplicative character* of S
if M_c(G ∗ H) = M_c(G) ⊗ M_c(H) for all graphs G, H, where
M_c(G) = c_E I + c_A A + c_N (J − I − A). Exactly six products have one, each
exactly one:

| product | code | c = (c_E, c_A, c_N) | M_c(G) |
|---|---|---|---|
| direct | 8 | (0, 1, 0) | A |
| complement-dual of direct | 127 | (0, 0, 1) | J − I − A = A(Ḡ) |
| strong | 13 | (1, 1, 0) | I + A |
| co-normal | 93 | (1, 0, 1) | J − A |
| symmetric difference | 85 | (1, −1, 1) | J − 2A = S(G) + I |
| {EA AE AA NN} | 141 | (1, 1, −1) | 2I + 2A − J = I − S(G) |

(S(G) = J − I − 2A is the Seidel matrix.) Every product with a multiplicative
character is associative and commutative.

*Proof.* By (0), M_c(G ∗ H) = c_E I⊗I + Σ_{(r,s)∈S} c_A R_r⊗R_s
+ Σ_{(r,s)∉S, ≠(E,E)} c_N R_r⊗R_s, while M_c(G) ⊗ M_c(H) =
Σ_{r,s} c_r c_s R_r(G) ⊗ R_s(H). The nine blocks have disjoint supports and are
all nonzero for G = H = P₃, so the identity holds for all G, H iff
c_E² = c_E and c_r c_s = c_A for (r,s) ∈ S, c_r c_s = c_N for the other cells.
*Case c_E = 0.* The cells (E, ·), (·, E) give 0 ∈ {c_A, c_N}. If c_N = 0 ≠ c_A,
these cells are outside S, AA gives c_A² ∈ {c_A, 0} so c_A = 1 and AA ∈ S, and
AN, NA, NN (value 0 = c_N) are outside S: S = {AA}. If c_A = 0 ≠ c_N, the cells
with an E and AA, AN, NA lie in S and NN gives c_N² ∈ {0, c_N}, so c_N = 1 and
NN ∉ S: S = all cells but NN (code 127).
*Case c_E = 1.* Cell EA gives c_A ∈ {c_A, c_N}: as c_A ≠ c_N, EA ∈ S; cell EN
gives c_N, so EN ∉ S; likewise AE ∈ S, NE ∉ S. With y = c_A, z = c_N the
remaining cells give the eight sign patterns of
y² ∈ {y, z}, yz ∈ {y, z}, z² ∈ {y, z} (the first option meaning "the cell is
in S"). Solving each pattern with y ≠ z: (y², yz, z²) = (y, y, y): y ∈ {0,1},
then z = y — none. (y, y, z): y = 0, z = 1 — co-normal. (y, z, y): y = 1,
z = −1 — code 141. (y, z, z): y = 1, z = 0 — strong. (z, y, y): y³ = y and
y⁴ = y force y ∈ {0, 1}, z = y — none. (z, y, z): z = y² and y(z − 1) = 0 give
y = −1, z = 1 — symmetric difference. (z, z, y): y²(y − 1) = 0, z = y — none.
(z, z, z): z ∈ {0, 1} and y = z — none. Conversely each listed c satisfies the
system. A multiplicative character gives associativity and commutativity
because ⊗ is associative and commutative up to the canonical permutations and
M_c determines the graph (c_A ≠ c_N). ∎ The system was also solved over ℂ by
sympy for all 256 codes (EXP-C14-03: identical result, numeric check of the
matrix identity on 30 random pairs per product) and, restricted to values in
{−1, 0, 1}, by kernel computation in Lean (`character_codes`).

## Theorem 5 (CLM-C14-05) — additive characters; every non-trivial commutative associative product has a character

An *additive character* satisfies M_c(G ∗ H) = M_c(G) ⊗ I + I ⊗ M_c(H). Exactly
two products have one: Cartesian (c = (0, t, 0), M = tA) and its
complement-dual 221 (c = (0, 0, t), M = t A(Ḡ)), t ≠ 0. Consequently each of the
ten commutative associative products except the empty and the complete product
has exactly one kind of character (H-C14-6).

*Proof.* As in Theorem 4 the identity is equivalent to 2c_E = c_E (cell EE),
c_s = value of cell (E, s), c_r = value of cell (r, E), and 0 = value of every
cell without an E. So c_E = 0, and 0 ∈ {c_A, c_N}. If c_N = 0 ≠ c_A: the cells
without E are outside S, EA, AE ∈ S, EN, NE ∉ S — the Cartesian product. If
c_A = 0 ≠ c_N: the cells without E are in S, EA, AE ∈ S, EN, NE ∉ S — code 221.
The empty and complete products have no character: for additive characters and
for multiplicative ones with c_E = 1, the cells EA and EN have the same status
and force c_A = c_N; for c_E = 0 the cells force 0 = c_A (complete) or
0 = c_N (empty), and then the cell AA (empty: c_A² = c_N = 0) resp. NN
(complete: c_N² = c_A = 0) forces c_A = c_N = 0. ∎ (sympy: EXP-C14-03.)

*Remark.* Characters turn graphs into quantities: spec M_c(G ∗ H) is the
multiset of products (resp. sums) of spec M_c(G) and spec M_c(H), so for each
of the eight products the characteristic polynomial of M_c is a homomorphism to
a "multiplicative" (resp. "additive") algebra of root multisets; det M_c and
tr M_c^k multiply (resp. traces of exponentials multiply).

## Theorem 6 (CLM-C14-06) — the symmetric difference is the Kronecker product of Seidel matrices

For all graphs G, H:  S(G ⊕ H) + I = (S(G) + I) ⊗ (S(H) + I), where ⊕ is the
symmetric difference product ((g,h) ~ (g',h') iff exactly one of g ~ g',
h ~ h' holds). Hence

1. the Seidel eigenvalues of G ⊕ H are (θ + 1)(η + 1) − 1 over the Seidel
   eigenvalues θ of G and η of H (with multiplicity);
2. ⊕ is compatible with Seidel switching: if G' is switching-equivalent to G
   and H' to H, then G' ⊕ H' is switching-equivalent to G ⊕ H. Thus ⊕ is a
   commutative, associative product on two-graphs (switching classes) with unit
   the class of K₁.

*Proof.* This is Theorem 4 for c = (1, −1, 1): entrywise,
(−1)^{a ⊕ b} = (−1)^a (−1)^b with a, b ∈ {0,1} the adjacency indicators
(0 on the diagonal). (1) is the spectrum of a Kronecker product.
(2) Switching with respect to Z multiplies S + I on both sides by the diagonal
±1 matrix D_Z; D_Z(S(G)+I)D_Z ⊗ D_W(S(H)+I)D_W = (D_Z ⊗ D_W)(S(G⊕H)+I)(D_Z ⊗ D_W),
and D_Z ⊗ D_W is again a diagonal ±1 matrix, i.e. a switching of G ⊕ H;
permutations behave in the same way. ∎ (Lean, relation level: `xor_character`;
integer check of the identity for all pairs of graphs with ≤ 3 vertices and
numerical check of (1) on 30 random pairs: EXP-C14-06, EXP-C14-03.)

## Theorem 7 (CLM-C14-07) — complete bipartite graphs, and K₂ absorbs switching

(a) K_{a,b} ⊕ K_{c,d} ≅ K_{ac+bd, ad+bc} (with K_{m,0} the edgeless graph).
Hence G ↦ (|V(G)|, |a − b|) maps the complete bipartite graphs K_{a,b}
multiplicatively into ℕ × ℕ: orders multiply and imbalances multiply.
(b) For a ≥ 1 and any graph X and Z ⊆ V(X): K_{a,a} ⊕ X ≅ K_{a,a} ⊕ X^Z
(X^Z = X switched with respect to Z).
(c) K₂ ⊕ X ≅ K₂ ⊕ Y iff X and Y are switching-equivalent.

*Proof.* (a) S(K_{a,b}) + I = wwᵀ with w = (1^a, (−1)^b); by Theorem 6,
S(K_{a,b} ⊕ K_{c,d}) + I = (w ⊗ u)(w ⊗ u)ᵀ. A graph with S + I = vvᵀ,
v ∈ {±1}ⁿ, is the complete bipartite graph between {v = 1} and {v = −1}; here
w ⊗ u has ac + bd entries +1 and ad + bc entries −1.
(b) Let σ be the involution of V(K_{a,a}) exchanging the two sides, so that
w_{σ(g)} = −w_g. Define Π(g, x) = (σ(g), x) for x ∈ Z and Π(g, x) = (g, x)
otherwise. Then the matrix entry of K_{a,a} ⊕ X at (Π⁻¹(g,x), Π⁻¹(g',y)) is
w_g w_{g'} d_x d_y (S(X)+I)_{xy} with d = ±1 the indicator of Z, which is the
entry of K_{a,a} ⊕ X^Z at ((g,x),(g',y)). So Π is an isomorphism.
(c) "If" is (b) with a = 1. "Only if": call vertices u, v *twins* if the rows
of M = S + I at u and v are equal up to sign. Twinness is an equivalence
relation invariant under switching and permutation, so a switching class
determines its twin-free *reduct* R (one vertex per class; well defined up to
switching) and the class sizes m : V(R) → ℕ up to automorphisms of R;
conversely (R, m) determines the class (switch each class to equal rows). In
J₂ ⊗ M the rows at (0, u) and (1, u) are equal and (g, u), (g', v) are twins
iff u, v are; so the reduct of [2K₁ ⊕ X] is the reduct of [X] with multiplicities
doubled. If K₂ ⊕ X ≅ K₂ ⊕ Y then [K₂ ⊕ X] = [K₂ ⊕ Y], and since K₂ ~ 2K₁,
[2K₁ ⊕ X] = [2K₁ ⊕ Y]; hence (R_X, 2m_X) ≅ (R_Y, 2m_Y), so (R_X, m_X) ≅ (R_Y, m_Y)
and X ~ Y. ∎ (EXP-C14-05: 12,713 pairs of graphs with ≤ 6 vertices,
0 violations of (c); EXP-C14-06: exact checks of (a) for a, b, c, d ≤ 4 and of
(b) for K₂ on 200 random cases.)

## Theorem 8 (CLM-C14-08, refutes H-C14-3) — the symmetric difference does not factor uniquely, even on connected graphs

(a) C₄ = K₂ ⊕ K₂ = K₂ ⊕ 2K₁. (b) K₂ ⊕ K₂ ⊕ K₂ ≅ K₂ ⊕ K_{1,3}, where K₂ and
K_{1,3} are connected and prime for ⊕ (in the monoid of all graphs and in that
of connected graphs). (c) 3K₁ ⊕ K_{2,3} ≅ K_{1,2} ⊕ K_{1,4} ≅ K_{6,9}.

*Proof.* (a) and (c) are Theorem 7(a): (2,0)·(2,0) = (2,0)·(2,2) = (4,0) and
(3,3)·(5,1) = (3,1)·(5,3) = (15,3) in the (order, imbalance) coordinates.
(b) C₄ = K₂ ⊕ K₂ and K_{1,3} are switching-equivalent (switch the centre of the
claw: 4K₁; switch two opposite vertices of C₄: 4K₁), so Theorem 7(b) gives
K₂ ⊕ C₄ ≅ K₂ ⊕ K_{1,3}. Primality of K_{1,3}: the products of two graphs of
order 2 are C₄ (three ways) and 4K₁. The two factorisations {K₂, K₂, K₂} and
{K₂, K_{1,3}} differ. ∎ (Explicit isomorphism found by exhaustive search,
EXP-C14-06; census in EXP-C14-04: non-unique XOR factorisations of connected
graphs at orders 8, 10, 12, 14 — 2, 6, 15, 53 classes.)

## Theorem 9 (CLM-C14-09) — Cartesian and strong products give isomorphic graded monoids

Let 𝒢 be the set of isomorphism classes of finite graphs. Then (𝒢, □) and
(𝒢, ⊠) are isomorphic monoids by an isomorphism preserving the number of
vertices; so are (𝒢, ∨) (co-normal) and (𝒢, ★) (code 221) by complementation.
In particular the numbers of prime graphs of each order coincide for all four
products, and the numbers of connected □-primes and connected ⊠-primes coincide
(OEIS A129582).

*Proof.* Both products distribute over disjoint union and have unit K₁, and
connected graphs have unique prime factorisation into connected primes
(Sabidussi 1960 / Vizing 1963 for □; Dörfler–Imrich 1970 / McKenzie 1971 for
⊠). A graph is the disjoint union of its components, so (𝒢, +, □) is the
polynomial semiring ℕ[X_□] in the connected □-primes and likewise
(𝒢, +, ⊠) ≅ ℕ[X_⊠]. Let pₙ (resp. qₙ) be the number of connected □-primes
(⊠-primes) of order n. Unique factorisation of connected graphs gives
Σₙ cₙ n^{-s} = Πₙ (1 − n^{-s})^{−pₙ} = Πₙ (1 − n^{-s})^{−qₙ} with cₙ the number
of connected graphs of order n; comparing coefficients inductively gives
pₙ = qₙ. Any order-preserving bijection X_□ → X_⊠ extends to a semiring
isomorphism ℕ[X_□] → ℕ[X_⊠] preserving the order (evaluation of a polynomial
at the orders of its variables). Complementation is an isomorphism (𝒢, ⊠) →
(𝒢, ∨) and (𝒢, □) → (𝒢, ★) since these are complement-conjugate (Theorem 2). ∎
This depends on the cited published theorems; the census EXP-C14-04 confirms
equal prime counts for orders ≤ 15 and reproduces A129582 to n = 15.

## Theorem 11 (CLM-C14-11) — a lexicographic collision at order 10

2K₁[C₄ + K₁] ≅ (2K₂ + K₁)[2K₁] ≅ 2C₄ + 2K₁, with C₄ + K₁ and 2K₂ + K₁ prime;
no collision of multisets of prime factors occurs for the lexicographic
product below order 10.

*Proof.* 2K₁[X] is two disjoint copies of X, so
2K₁[C₄ + K₁] = 2C₄ + 2K₁; Y[2K₁] replaces every vertex of Y by two
non-adjacent twins, so K₂[2K₁] = C₄ and K₁[2K₁] = 2K₁, whence
(2K₂ + K₁)[2K₁] = 2C₄ + 2K₁. Graphs of prime order are prime. Minimality is
the exact census EXP-C14-04 (orders 4, 6, 8, 9: no collision). ∎
